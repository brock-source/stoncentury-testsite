# What If You Do Not Qualify for Infinite Banking?

Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VyIyVLRXQss

## Transcript

Most people were taught to follow the rules with money: save it, spend it, borrow it, pay interest, and hope retirement works out. But what if the rules were written by the very system keeping you stuck? Welcome to Cashflow Legendz, the show where Nate, Brandon, and Brock challenge conventional financial thinking, expose the hidden leaks in your cash flow, and help families and business owners build more control, certainty, and legacy. This show is for educational purposes only, not financial, tax, or legal advice.

The hosts open by sharing what they're each grateful for that week — Brandon talks about conviction from a Sunday sermon about not making excuses for small compromises, Brock is thankful for warmer weather, and Nate is thankful for a business partner who holds him accountable and for time spent watching his son at soccer practice.

They then turn to a section in Nelson Nash's Becoming Your Own Banker that asks, "What if I am uninsurable?" The deeper question, they argue, is whether people are even asking the right question in the first place. Nelson wrote the book for the layperson, not for insurance professionals, and he didn't want anyone to reach the end of the book and conclude the concept wasn't for them just because they couldn't personally qualify for a policy. Being uninsurable doesn't disqualify someone from applying these principles in their life.

Brock notes that people need to accept that being uninsurable is a real possibility — he's currently working with a breadwinner client who wanted to put a policy on his wife instead because her health ratings were better, which is ill-advised. Brock would rather a client have a less efficient policy than none at all. Nate explains underwriting in simple terms: insurance companies use actuarial charts based on height, weight, and health to assign a rating (preferred, standard, etc.), and there are no "deals" in insurance — just the average cost. Getting qualified and understanding the need for banking matters more than the specific rating you land.

Nate shares an example of a client whose health had improved after previously being declined, so he came back to get qualified — like getting pre-approved for a home loan, once you know what you're capable of, you want as much as you can get for the protection and opportunity it creates. Brandon adds an anecdote about a client who calculated that 41% of his payments in 2025 went to interest — even higher than the 34.5% Nelson cited in the book — reinforcing the idea that redirecting money away from interest into a system you control matters more than obsessing over your insurance rating.

The group discusses why people disqualify themselves too early: confusion over whose life to insure first (self, spouse, or kids), when in almost all cases barring uninsurability the breadwinner's income should be protected first, since every breadwinner wants their income replaced for as long as possible if they were no longer there. Brandon notes that insurance companies view applicants as their "product," carefully underwritten based on decades of actuarial data — and if your health improves (quitting tobacco, losing weight, years cancer-free), insurers will happily re-rate you as a better version of that product. This can motivate people to make health changes, like quitting tobacco once they see the cost difference in black and white.

Nate adds that people often disqualify themselves believing they don't have the money to fund a policy, when in fact repositioning existing income and assets — rather than adding new out-of-pocket cost — frequently makes it possible. Brock emphasizes that this is almost always an "order of operations" problem: for example, someone maxing out a 401(k) while carrying high-interest debt would get a better return by pausing retirement contributions and paying off debt first. He compares it to a chiropractor tracing a knee problem back to a weak core — get to the foundational issue and everything downstream improves. Brock frames money as something that is always flowing somewhere, whether people intend it or not, and repositioning dollars without new out-of-pocket cost is a core principle they try to implement with every client.

They cover "insurable interest" — the financial connection between people that allows one to hold a policy on another, applying to spouses, kids, and in some cases nephews, nieces, grandkids, and business partners. Brock outlines two clear-cut business scenarios: key-person coverage on an employee critical to the business, and buy-sell agreements funded by life insurance so that if a business partner dies, the surviving partner can buy out the deceased's shares rather than end up in business with the partner's spouse. Brock also describes a coal-business client who used to finance a new truck every year and pay it off quickly with cash flow, even though he had the cash on hand — instead, he now buys the truck with a policy loan and repays the loan with the same cash flow the truck generates, recapitalizing his bank.

Nate ties this back to Nelson's point (page 3 of the book) that the book "is not about investments... it's about how one finances the things of life" — the only thing that changes is where you store your wealth as you build it into a policy. The conversation turns to reframing the word "premium": traditionally seen as a pure cost, but once you understand the system, premium becomes an opportunity, especially since cost-basis rules mean the total premiums paid become tax-free withdrawable amounts later in retirement. Brandon shares that he and Nate personally had to work through the tension of resenting premiums in the traditional sense while also wanting to pay as much premium as possible into their own policies — resolving it by recognizing that insurability only decreases with time, so paying premium while you can is a privilege, not just a cost.

Closing out, the hosts pose Nelson's idea that "if nothing changes until your thinking changes, nothing changes," and encourage listeners to ask whether an advisor might see something in their financial situation they can't see themselves. Brandon cites a statistic that 66% of people aren't actively working with any financial advisor, and stresses that he, Nate, and Brock don't charge for an initial conversation — the real cost isn't fees, it's being unwilling to invest in yourself by rethinking how you think about money. They close by inviting listeners to reach out via cashflowlegendz.com or their social channels.
